The inspiration for this show, he mentioned, was Johannes Kepler's book *The Six-Cornered Snowflake*, in which the 17th-century German astronomer ponders deep questions in mathematics and physics through the symmetrically arranged nature of a snowflake.

Zooming in from the scale of snowflakes down to the unimaginably miniature world of quarks, Professor Cox explained the presence of tightly packed and organised patterns in nature. Stepping back from this and zooming out towards the solar system, galaxies, and the entire observable universe, he explored how similar patterns exist at all scales, and demonstrated how we have been able to observe these patterns with modern scientific instruments.

The talk covered a wide variety of topics as seen through a physics lens: the influence of quantum mechanics in the large-scale models of physics, the emergence of life in the universe, The Great Silence / [Fermi paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi_paradox), [Great Filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Filter), theory of [inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_inflation), [bubble universes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eternal_inflation), and a lot more.

The show also featured breathtaking photos from the various space telescopes, the Voyager missions, and the recent Artemis mission. The soundtrack included [Mahler's second symphony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_No._2_(Mahler)) and [music written for the show](https://pjharvey.bandcamp.com/album/voyager) by PJ Harvey. Professor Cox even played a keytar briefly to demonstrate harmonics—to explain the behaviour of electrons with a musical metaphor—and played the opening riff of [*The Final Countdown*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Final_Countdown_(song)) as a nod to the time when his band opened for *Europe* back in his musician days.

It was heartening to see a massive sports stadium filled with science enthusiasts on a Friday night. There may be hope for us yet.
