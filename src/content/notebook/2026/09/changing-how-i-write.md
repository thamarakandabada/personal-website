Having just returned from a run I find myself writing as if in a frenzy. I'm working on another post for this month's [Bear Blog Carnival](https://jamesg.blog/2026/09/02/music-memories-announcement);  it's got me very excited. This post requires some work. Not just research—that is, in fact, minimal—but a good amount of care, too. Mostly, it requires the time it deserves to flourish and take the final form I am envisioning. I'm writing about a piece of music I love, and I want to do it justice.

Runs are great for getting my thoughts together. I still feel guilty for skipping my long run on Sunday; my knees had been hurting for a day or two and I thought it best to give it a miss. I'm back in usual form today, having just completed a 5K.

On my run today I was thinking about how I write at this point in my life. The process and the tools that I use have changed a lot over the years, but I think I have finally settled on a comfortable routine and pace, with tools that serve me well.

## Tools

Ideas take form on [Bear](/notebook/2026/08/back-to-bear) first; it's where I take my notes. I recently went through all my older notes and organised them with tags. All the new post ideas for my blog now live in one section. I just have to pull my phone out and write a few lines down when inspiration hits. I never know when that's going to happen—on the bus, on my lunch break, in the cinema before my film starts—but I'm ready when it does.

The notes from Bear then get copied to the new [Astro Editor](/notebook/2026/09/astro-editor). It helps that they're both Markdown-based writing apps. Astro Editor gives me a completely distraction-free environment; a blank screen with just my words on it. No menus, no frontmatter, no folder structure. All that comes later, when it's time to publish.

Once the post is written out and the final editing pass is made, I turn to VS Code to push the changes to my live site.

## Wasting time

I'm changing the environment that I write in with the help of my new tools, too. Over the last few months, with my Astro website, I used to have a preview of the post open in a local dev environment on one side of the screen while I wrote in VS Code on the other. I was constantly looking back and forth between the two. I must have enjoyed some sense of security and/or joy seeing in real time that my words looked good on the website.

I was looking up synonyms, meanings of words, and spelling, too, as I wrote. I've always been impatient and restless in most things I do, and it clearly made its way into my writing. Instead of letting things breathe and letting my brain work things out on its own, I was frantically looking for new words, optimising the design, and doing constant editing passes, because staring at a screen without typing incessantly seemed unproductive somehow. [If I wasn't in motion, I was wasting time](https://dice.camp/@doyce/117224477061183017). That's how it felt.

It's time for a different approach.

## A blank page can be scary

Perhaps my ever-changing focus had to do with the fact that I am so averse to the sight of a blank page.

I am not a writer by trade; my life does not depend on it, and this is only a very private and inconsequential blog. There is no real pressure to write consistently, and I doubt anyone would notice if I suddenly stopped writing. But it's an outlet, and it's important to me. Writing is probably the only way I know how to express myself fully, and a blank page, even when I set out to write something as silly as [a summary of my week](/notebook/2026/08/mid-week-notes), can look like a failure.

Not being allowed [to be bored](/notebook/2026/04/boredom) growing up has had a major effect on how I see the *value* of my time. Years of conditioning is difficult to unlearn. It's easy to push aside that horrible feeling of uselessness by simply darting across browser tabs *trying* to write. 

## Letting ideas breathe

Changing how I write will have to start with changing how I think about time. I must leave room for ideas to blossom and take shape, even when I find myself staring at a mostly blank screen with a few half-written sentences on it. Finding my own words will take time, but I must learn how not to seek refuge in a thesaurus every time a sentence seems "too plain". In an age when people are just too eager to use genAI tools to instantly produce vapid walls of text, I must trust in my ability to write my own words with conviction, clarity, and most importantly, patience.
