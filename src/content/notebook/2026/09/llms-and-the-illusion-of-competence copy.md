The other day, in a business setting, I was listening to someone talking about an app they had vibe-coded. I have to leave out names and any other identifying details, but this person was claiming to have made a tool that was orders of magnitude better than anything they had used before. This magic app, according to them, was miles ahead of anything that is currently commercially available. Mentioning how it only took them approximately 7 hours over a weekend to make this tool from scratch, they went on to wax philosophical about how business was changing rapidly due to AI. Their takeaway: we should all be using AI. But what really caught my attention was that they were making a sales pitch for this hastily put together tool, pitching it as a product ready to be sold to others in the industry. The term *Minimum Viable Product* was used.

This made me think about Thomas' excellent blog post titled [*Four Cornerstones of Development*](https://www.vanderwal.net/random/entrysel.php?blog=2185). I had read it only days before, so the content was fresh in my mind. Very simply put, any app or software solution, to be an actually viable and sellable product, should be:

1. Usable

2. Reliable

3. Maintainable

4. Repeatable

Thomas goes to great detail in explaining what these concepts mean in his post. That, and his follow up post [*Vibe Markets Don’t Always Rhyme with Good Product*](https://www.vanderwal.net/random/entrysel.php?blog=2187) are well worth a read. In the *Four Cornerstones* post he says:

>With the increase of vibe coding and people creating products for “unique” purposes, but then people are sharing these more widely than personal use, the need for understanding and considering what they are getting into is really needed.

It would be hypocritical of me to discount all vibe-coded apps as useless or somehow inferior when I have [made these](/notebook/2026/03/building-a-physical-media-showcase-with-lovable) myself. But never in a million years did I think, "I should sell this". The tools I made were for personal use only, and I open-sourced and published the code in case someone else may find it useful. I am fully aware these may be full of security vulnerabilities, bugs, and scalability issues, but these aren't significant concerns given the scope and use case of my apps. But the stakes are different for a commercial enterprise.

So when someone openly and rather callously calls for customers for a product they spent 7 hours making, alarm bells should ring. (Whether you could call vibe-coding "making" is another matter—I have some thoughts on this [here](/notebook/2026/08/did-i-make-this)). When I mentioned this to a friend, he remarked that this false confidence problem was inevitable now that coding has essentially been democratised (his wording).

This incident has convinced me that we should all be exercising utmost scrutiny with the digital tools we choose to hand our lives over to, especially if they come from overconfident salesmen. Perhaps ask them what the four cornerstones of development are; if they've never heard of them, run away.
