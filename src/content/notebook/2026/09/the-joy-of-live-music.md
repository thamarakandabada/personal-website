***

Almost to the day four years ago, having packed our life into a few bags and moved across continents, we landed in a new country. We didn't know anyone in Belfast, or in Northern Ireland, when we moved here. The cause of the move may have been higher education but it felt like many other things drew us here, too. Sri Lanka had been going through a rough patch then. While our lives were quite comfortable compared to most, the prospect of a new and freer life elsewhere had been appealing for some time, and we were fortunate enough—with help from our parents—to be able to afford it.

My life has changed drastically since we arrived. Back in Sri Lanka, I was mostly confined to my home even as an adult, seldom venturing out save for the obligatory grocery shop runs and family visits. Of course, I saw my friends, went to the cinema every now and then to see the latest blockbuster, and went out for meals, but those days were few and far between. I had grown accustomed to being a homebody—growing up without much freedom may have had something to do with it, but so did the sweltering heat and the lack of reliable public transport. More than anything, though, it felt like there wasn't anything worth going through the hassle of venturing outside to see.

This is not an indictment on the Sri Lankan cultural landscape—that wouldn't be fair. We have a vibrant theatre and music scene, plenty of independent artists who put themselves out there, and a largely sympathetic population who support the arts. But for reasons explained above I couldn't quite get over the initial hurdle of leaving the house.

Things changed for me in Belfast. I can't remember when exactly, but I found myself attending more and more events; live performances of all kinds: music, theatre, public talks, and more. I started documenting them on my website, too. Music is by far the most popular type. I learned to be comfortable, and invisible, in large crowds, taking in only the boundless euphoria that followed every musical note and none of the anxiety of being in a social gathering. I sought artists that I've loved for many years and some new ones, too, travelling long distances to see them (frequently to England and Scotland) almost every month. I cherished, and still do, the early morning flights on weekends, hours killed at airports, and braving all kinds of weather standing until my feet hurt waiting for a gig to start. I enjoy the ritual of walking right up to the stage, or as close as I can get, to look at all the gear neatly laid out, ready to be played.

Normally, I keep to myself at these. There have been occasions where I've risked a conversation or two—the [Holy Holy gig in Liverpool](/gigs/2025/holy-holy) comes to mind. But that's Bowie fans for you; they are the nicest people on earth. I was made to feel welcome, and part of the gang, among a motley crew of young and old fans from Glasgow, York, and Liverpool, among other places. We talked music all night. The band was magical. The Starman was alive that night; we felt him.

But even the gigs where I talk to nobody at all are etched in my mind as wonderful memories. It doesn't matter how many times I've seen it, the very first second the band walks on stage to thundering applause and cheering from the crowd is always a powerful moment. The first beat of the drum always carries an energy I can never explain in words. The electronic hum of a guitar never ceases to put me in a trance. Never in these four years have I left a gig without a smile on my face. The sweaty crowds and the smell of beer don't bother me; they recede into the background as the extreme joy of having experienced something that I love takes over. It is this joy that has sustained me through physically taxing experiences like going to three massive rock gigs across two countries in three days, with a flight in between. Sure, my body ached for days after that wild weekend, but while I was standing there, in the crowd, screaming my heart out and swaying to the music, I never gave it a second thought.

I'm too much of a square to live a life of artistry, but I gladly appreciate those who do it. I don't have any illusions of becoming a musician myself, but music has always been a big part of my life, [even when I lacked the means to make my own](/notebook/2026/08/guitaring/). Nowadays, I depend on music so much that the thought of not having [another gig to look forward to](/gigs#upcoming-gigs) can be devastatingly bleak. I used to think that performatively sharing my musical memories with the world was important (back when I was on Instagram), but I've come to realise that making them is enough. They are for me.

Music memories are deeply personal. Growing up in Sri Lanka I would never have thought that one day I would get to experience my favourite music performed live in some of the biggest venues in the UK. Now that I have the opportunity to make these memories, I will cherish every single one.
